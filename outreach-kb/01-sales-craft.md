# 01 — Sales Craft

> **Product claims warning:** any description in this doc of what ReceptAI does (booking, texting the tech, recordings, transfers, setup time, number forwarding, scripts) is illustrative only. Before using it with a prospect, confirm it in **Product Truth**. If Product Truth doesn't confirm it, leave it out.


How to sell well, for any B2B product and any buyer. Follow these rules by default. Product- and ICP-specific sections of this knowledge base can override details (price, offer, who to target). They cannot override Section 10 (Ethics and compliance).

Examples use one running case: an AI receptionist that answers after-hours calls for US trade contractors (HVAC, plumbing, etc.), sold as a free 2-week pilot then a monthly fee. Swap in any product.

A note on data: numbers below come from vendor research (Gong, Lavender, Instantly) and classic studies. Vendor data is large but self-published and correlational. Treat it as a strong default, not physics. Your own test results (Section 11) beat any benchmark.

## 1. Core principles

What top B2B sellers do differently, and the rules that follow.

**Evidence**
- Top performers listen more than they talk. Gong found the best B2B calls run about 43% seller talk, 57% buyer talk ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/winning-sales-conversations)).
- Neil Rackham's team studied 35,000 sales calls over 12 years. Their finding, built into SPIN Selling: in larger sales, success comes from questions that develop the buyer's problem, not from closing tricks ([Wikipedia: Neil Rackham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Rackham); [Huthwaite](https://www.huthwaiteinternational.com/blog/rethinking-spin-the-universal-framework)).
- The Challenger research found nearly 40% of top performers were "Challengers" (teach the buyer something, tailor it, take control of the process), while only 7% were "Relationship Builders" ([Challenger Inc.](https://challengerinc.com/what-is-challenger-sales-methodology/)).
- Pitching too early hurts. Gong's cold email data showed pitch-heavy emails cut replies by up to 57% ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/does-cold-email-even-work-any-more-heres-what-the-data-says)).

**Rules**
- Always lead with the buyer's problem, not your product. The product is the answer to a question the buyer must ask first.
- Always earn the right to pitch by diagnosing first. No diagnosis, no prescription.
- Always bring one useful insight the buyer didn't have (a number, a pattern you see across similar companies, a cost they haven't counted).
- Always be willing to disqualify. A fast "this isn't a fit" is a good outcome. It saves both sides time and builds trust.
- Always control the process, not the person. Propose the next step, the agenda, and the timeline. Never pressure.
- Always tell the truth, including about limits. One caught exaggeration kills the deal and your sender reputation.
- Never argue with a buyer. Get curious instead.
- Never talk about yourself for long. Gong found win rates drop when the "about us" part runs past about two minutes ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/winning-sales-conversations)).

**Good**
- "Most shops your size we talk to lose a few after-hours calls a week to voicemail. Is that true for you, or have you solved it?"
- "Honestly, if you already have a live answering service you're happy with, this probably isn't worth switching for. What made you look?"

**Bad**
- "Our cutting-edge AI platform revolutionizes how contractors handle calls. Here are our 12 features..." (pitch before problem, buzzwords)
- "I promise this will double your revenue." (unprovable claim)

## 2. Cold outreach writing

### 2.1 Length and readability
- Keep first-touch emails under 80 words; aim for 25–75. Gong found emails under 100 words get the most replies, with the best around 3–4 sentences (~50 words) ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/does-cold-email-even-work-any-more-heres-what-the-data-says)). Lavender recommends under 75, ideally 25–50 ([Lavender](https://lavender.ai/blog/cold-email-101)). Instantly's 2026 benchmark recommends under 80 ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026)).
- Write at a 3rd–5th grade reading level. Lavender reports this gets 67% more replies, and that 70% of cold emails are written at 10th grade or above ([Lavender](https://lavender.ai/blog/cold-email-101)).
- Write for a phone screen. Lavender reports opens are 8x more likely on mobile and readers scan for about 11 seconds ([Lavender](https://lavender.ai/blog/cold-email-101)).
- Use short words, short sentences, one idea per line. No bullet lists, no bold, no images, no attachments in a first touch.

### 2.2 Subject lines
- Keep subject lines to 1–3 words, lowercase or sentence case ([Lavender](https://lavender.ai/blog/cold-email-101)).
- Make it look like an internal email from a colleague: a topic, not a pitch. Gong found buzzwords and numbers in subject lines can cut opens by up to 17.9%, and recommends avoiding questions and social proof in subject lines ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/does-cold-email-even-work-any-more-heres-what-the-data-says)).
- Never write a subject line that misrepresents the email (no fake "Re:", no "Your invoice"). This is also a legal requirement (Section 10).

Good: `after-hours calls` · `missed calls` · `{{company}} weekends`
Bad: `🚀 Boost Revenue 300% With AI!` · `Re: our call` (when there was no call) · `Quick question?`

### 2.3 Openers
- Open with the buyer's world: an observation about them or a problem common to their role. Never open with your name or company.
- Never open with "I hope this finds you well", "My name is...", or "I'm reaching out because...".
- Personalize only when the detail is real and relevant to the problem. A fake-personal line ("Loved your recent post!") is worse than none.

Good: "Saw you run 24/7 emergency service across three counties."
Good: "Most HVAC owners I talk to say the 9pm–7am calls are the ones they lose."
Bad: "Hope you're having a great week! I came across your profile and was impressed."

### 2.4 Body structure (the 4-line email)
1. **Observation** about them or their role.
2. **Problem** stated in their words, ideally with a cost.
3. **Proof or insight**, one line, specific (not "we help companies like yours").
4. **One soft CTA.**

### 2.5 Calls to action
- Use exactly one CTA per email. Never give options ("call, demo, or reply?").
- In cold email, prefer interest-based CTAs over meeting requests. Gong analyzed 304,174 emails: interest CTAs ("Interested in learning more?") succeeded ~47% vs ~40% for asking for a specific meeting time. But once a deal is active, specific-time CTAs won (37% vs 25% for interest CTAs) ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/this-surprising-cold-email-cta-will-help-you-book-a-lot-more-meetings)).
- Rule: **cold = ask for interest; warm/in-deal = ask for a specific time.**
- Make the CTA answerable with one word.

Good (cold): "Worth a look?" · "Open to seeing how it handled a real 2am call?" · "Is this a problem for you, or already solved?"
Good (in-deal): "Does Thursday at 10am Central work to set up the pilot?"
Bad (cold): "Can we hop on a 30-minute call this week? Here's my Calendly." · "Let me know if you'd like a demo, a case study, or a call."

### 2.6 Full example

**Good (58 words)**
> Subject: after-hours calls
>
> Hi Mike, saw Rivera Plumbing offers 24/7 emergency service.
>
> Most shops your size send night calls to voicemail, and those callers usually phone the next plumber on Google.
>
> We built a receptionist that answers those calls, books the job, and texts your on-call tech. Free for two weeks.
>
> Worth a look?
>
> Sam

**Bad (pitch dump)**
> Subject: Revolutionize Your Customer Experience With AI!
>
> Hi there, I hope this email finds you well! My name is Sam and I'm the Business Development Manager at NerdFlow, a leading provider of cutting-edge AI solutions. Our all-in-one platform leverages state-of-the-art technology to... [180 more words] ...Let me know a good time for a 30-minute demo!

### 2.7 Follow-ups in a sequence
- Send 4–7 touches total. Instantly's data shows 58% of replies come from the first email and 42% from follow-ups; returns fall off past seven steps unless each adds new value ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026)).
- Every follow-up must add something new: a different angle, a short insight, a relevant example, or a simpler ask. Never "bumping this to the top of your inbox."
- Keep follow-ups shorter than the first email.
- Space them out: roughly days 1, 3, 7, 14, 21 (unverified; a common practitioner pattern, test it).
- Reply in the same thread for follow-ups 1–2; a new thread with a new angle is fine later.

Good follow-up: "One more thought: a missed emergency call is often a $300–$1,500 job (your numbers will differ). If even one a month goes to voicemail, it adds up fast. Still worth a look?"
Bad follow-up: "Just checking in to see if you saw my last email?"

### 2.8 LinkedIn
- Connection requests: no pitch, or no note at all. Pitching in the connection note burns the connection.
- After connecting, wait for a natural reason to message (they posted, they viewed your profile, a mutual topic). Keep DMs under 50 words, one question.

## 3. Discovery

Goal: understand the buyer's situation well enough to decide (a) whether there is a real problem, (b) what it costs, (c) whether you can solve it, (d) who decides and when.

### 3.1 Frameworks
- **SPIN** (Rackham): Situation → Problem → Implication → Need-payoff. Keep Situation questions few (look up what you can beforehand). Spend most time on Implication and Need-payoff questions, which make the buyer state the cost and the value in their own words ([Huthwaite](https://www.huthwaiteinternational.com/blog/rethinking-spin-the-universal-framework)).
- **Challenger "teach"**: bring an insight that reframes the problem ("most shops think the problem is staffing; it's actually where the calls go after 6pm") ([Challenger Inc.](https://challengerinc.com/what-is-challenger-sales-methodology/)).
- **Tactical empathy** (Chris Voss, *Never Split the Difference*): use labels ("It sounds like weekends are the worst") and mirrors (repeat the last 1–3 words) to get people to elaborate. Use "no-oriented" questions ("Would it be crazy to test it for two weeks?") because people feel safer saying no ([summary](https://grahammann.net/book-notes/never-split-the-difference-chris-voss)).
- **Sandler-style qualification**: get pain, budget, and decision process on the table early; never chase someone with no pain (unverified as a formal citation; widely taught).

### 3.2 Rules
- Ask 11–14 targeted questions in a discovery call, spread through the conversation. Gong's analysis of 519,000+ calls found this range performed best, and that top performers spread questions out instead of front-loading an interrogation ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/nailing-your-sales-discovery-calls)).
- Ask one question at a time. Never stack three questions in one breath.
- After each answer, go one level deeper before changing topic ("What happens then?" "How often?" "What does that cost you?").
- Let the buyer say the problem and the cost in their own words. Then use their words back.
- Summarize before you pitch: "So if I heard you right: X, Y, costing about Z. Did I miss anything?"

### 3.3 Example questions (adapt to any product)

*Situation (keep few)*
1. How do you handle [the process] today?
2. Who's involved in that, and what tools do you use?
3. Roughly how many [calls / leads / orders / tickets] a week?

*Problem*
4. What's the most frustrating part of how that works now?
5. When does it break down? Give me the last time it happened.
6. What have you already tried to fix it?
7. If you could change one thing about it, what would it be?

*Implication*
8. When that happens, what does it cost you, in jobs, hours, or customers?
9. How often does that happen in a typical month?
10. Who else feels it? (customers, staff, you personally)
11. What happens if nothing changes over the next 6–12 months?
12. Has it ever cost you a big customer or a bad review?

*Need-payoff*
13. If that was solved, what would it be worth to you?
14. What would you do with the time or money you got back?
15. How would you know, in 30 days, that a fix was working?

*Process and fit*
16. Besides you, who would weigh in on a decision like this?
17. How have you bought similar tools or services before?
18. Is there a budget set aside for this, or would it come from somewhere else?
19. What would make this a definite no for you?
20. If it worked, what's your timeline to have it in place?

### 3.4 Quantifying the cost of the problem
- Always build the number with the buyer, using their inputs. Never invent their numbers.
- Formula: **frequency × value per incident × time period**, then sanity-check.
- Example: "You said about 4 after-hours calls a week go to voicemail, and maybe half of those were real jobs. Your average emergency job is about $600? So roughly 2 × $600 × 4 weeks ≈ $4,800 a month at risk. Even if we're off by half, is that about right?"
- Add soft costs only if they raise them (owner's sleep, tech burnout, reviews).
- Write the agreed number down. It anchors price later (Section 5).

## 4. Objection handling

### 4.1 The framework: Pause → Acknowledge → Clarify → Respond → Confirm
1. **Pause.** Don't rush to defend.
2. **Acknowledge** without agreeing or arguing: "That's fair." / "Makes sense."
3. **Clarify** with a question to find the real objection: "When you say X, what's behind that?"
4. **Respond** to the real concern, briefly, with a fact, an example, or a lower-risk option.
5. **Confirm**: "Does that address it, or is there something else?"

### 4.2 Finding the real objection
- The first objection is often a polite brush-off, not the real reason.
- Ask: "Is that the main thing, or is there something else making you hesitate?"
- Ask the isolating question: "If [objection] weren't an issue, would this be something you'd want?" If no, the objection isn't the real one.
- Use a label: "It sounds like you've been burned by something like this before." Then stay quiet.
- Gong found that risk-reducing language (opt-outs, guarantees, SLAs) was associated with a 32% higher win rate ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/winning-sales-conversations)). Lead with risk reversal when the objection is about risk.

### 4.3 Common objections

**"Not interested."**
- Respect it. Ask one light question, then stop.
- Good: "Totally fair. Is that because it's already handled, or just not a priority right now?" (If no reply or another no: stop and tag it.)
- Bad: "But you haven't even seen what we can do!" / sending three more emails.

**"Send me some info."**
- Often a polite exit. Qualify before sending.
- Good: "Happy to. So I send the right thing: is it [problem A] or [problem B] you'd care about more?" Then send something short (1 page or a 2-minute example) and propose a specific next step.
- Bad: attaching a 30-page deck and "let me know your thoughts!"

**"We already have something."**
- Be curious, not competitive. Never trash the alternative.
- Good: "Good, then you know the problem matters. How's it working for after-hours calls specifically? Anything you wish it did?"
- Good: "If it's working, keep it. Some people use us as a free 2-week side-by-side to compare."
- Bad: "Our product is way better than theirs."

**"Too expensive."**
- Clarify: expensive compared to what? Budget, value, or a competitor?
- Re-anchor to the cost of the problem they agreed (Section 3.4).
- Good: "Fair. Compared to what? ... You mentioned roughly $4,800 a month in missed jobs. If it saved even one job a month, would it pay for itself?"
- Bad: "OK, I can do 30% off." (See Section 5.)

**"Bad timing / reach out later."**
- Find out if it's real (busy season, budget cycle) or a soft no.
- Good: "Makes sense. What's going on right now? ... When would be better: after [event] in [month]?" Then get permission: "OK if I check back on [specific date]?" and schedule it.
- Bad: "No problem!" and then emailing again next week.

**"I need to talk to my partner / boss."**
- Help them sell it internally. Offer to join.
- Good: "Of course. What do you think they'll ask? ... Would it help if I joined a 15-minute call with both of you, or should I send a 3-line summary you can forward?" Then set a date: "When are you talking to them?"
- Bad: "Sure, let me know!" (no next step, deal stalls).

## 5. Price conversations

### 5.1 When to state price
- Earn the price conversation with value first. Gong found top reps discuss price near the end of calls, while weaker reps scatter it throughout; buyer price questions 3–4 times per call correlated with the highest win rates ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/sales-reps-talk-pricing)).
- If asked about price early, never dodge. Give a straight range, then return to fit: "It's about $X a month, cancel anytime. Whether that's worth it depends on how many calls you're missing. Can I ask a couple of questions first?"
- In cold email, a clear simple price or a free trial can be stated if it lowers friction. Never hide price in a way that feels evasive.

### 5.2 Anchoring
- Always state price next to the cost of the problem the buyer agreed to: "You estimated $4,800 a month at risk. This is $497 a month."
- Compare to the realistic alternative, not to zero (an employee, an answering service, the buyer's own time).
- State price plainly and confidently, then stop talking. Don't apologize or pad it.

### 5.3 Discounting
- Never discount on the first ask. A fast discount signals the price was fake.
- Never offer a discount nobody asked for.
- If you give something, get something: a longer commitment, annual prepay, a case study, a referral, a faster start. "If you can commit to annual, I can do X."
- Prefer reducing risk over reducing price: a longer pilot, an easy cancel, a performance check-in.
- Only offer concessions the business has pre-approved. Never invent pricing.

### 5.4 "Can you do it cheaper?"
- Good: "I get it. Before we talk price, is the issue the total, or not being sure it'll pay off?" (If pay-off: offer the pilot or a check-in. If total: offer a smaller scope, not a lower rate.)
- Good: "The price is the price for the full setup. What I can do is [pre-approved trade]."
- Bad: "Let me see what I can do... how about half?"
- Bad: "This price is only good until Friday!" (false urgency; see Section 10)

## 6. Closing and next steps

### 6.1 Rules
- Never end a conversation without a specific next step: **what, who, and when (date and time zone)**.
- Ask for the next step directly. "What would you like to do next?" is weaker than proposing one.
- Use specific-time asks once the buyer is engaged (Gong: specific-time CTAs win in-deal) ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/this-surprising-cold-email-cta-will-help-you-book-a-lot-more-meetings)).
- Confirm in writing within the same day: recap, agreed next step, date.
- Use a mutual action plan for anything with more than one step: a short shared list of what each side does by when.

Good: "Let's do this: I'll set up the pilot by Wednesday, you forward your after-hours line Thursday, and we look at results together on the 22nd at 10am Central. Work?"
Bad: "Great chatting! Let me know when you're ready."

### 6.2 Mutual action plan (template)
| Step | Owner | Date |
|---|---|---|
| Kickoff call: confirm success criteria | Both | Day 0 |
| Setup and configuration | Seller | Day 1–2 |
| Go live | Buyer | Day 3 |
| Mid-pilot check-in | Both | Day 7 |
| Results review and decision | Both | Day 14 |

### 6.3 Trial / pilot to paid
- Agree on success criteria before the trial starts: "If it answers X calls and books Y jobs in two weeks, is that a yes?" Write it down.
- Book the results review call on the calendar at kickoff, not at the end.
- Get the buyer to a first real "win" in the first days (first booked job, first solved ticket). Show it to them.
- Check in mid-trial with a short summary of what happened, in numbers.
- At the review: show results against the agreed criteria, then ask directly: "Based on this, want to keep it running?"
- If the results are weak, say so honestly and diagnose. Never inflate trial results.
- Make the paid step easy: one link, clear terms, cancel anytime if that is the offer.
(These are common SaaS practices; no single controlled study is cited here.)

## 7. Follow-up cadence and when to stop

### 7.1 Cold sequences
- 4–7 touches over 3–4 weeks, each adding value ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026)).
- End with a polite close-the-loop email that makes "no" easy: "Sounds like this isn't a priority, so I'll stop here. If after-hours calls become a headache, just reply."
- After a sequence ends with no reply, wait at least 60–90 days before a new sequence with a new angle (unverified; common practice).

### 7.2 Active deals (after a conversation)
- Follow up within 24 hours of every call with a recap and next step.
- If the buyer goes quiet: follow up at roughly 2, 5, and 10 business days, each with a reason (new info, a question, a simpler option) (unverified; practitioner norm).

### 7.3 Stop immediately when
- They unsubscribe or say stop, remove, "not interested" twice, or anything hostile.
- They say they are the wrong person (ask once for the right person, then stop).
- The email bounces or the company is clearly out of business or out of scope.
- Stopping is required by law for opt-outs (Section 10). Never re-add an opted-out contact under another campaign.

## 8. Replying to inbound replies

### 8.1 Speed
- Reply to positive and question replies as fast as possible, ideally within 5 minutes during business hours, and same business day at the latest.
- Evidence (from inbound web leads, applies directionally): an MIT/InsideSales study found the odds of qualifying a lead dropped 21x if response took 30 minutes instead of 5 ([study PDF](https://25649.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na2.net/hub/25649/file-13535879-pdf/docs/mit_study.pdf)); HBR found firms that responded within an hour were 7x more likely to qualify the lead than those that waited longer ([HBR](https://hbr.org/2011/03/the-short-life-of-online-sales-leads)).

### 8.2 Tone and length
- Match their length and energy. A two-line reply gets a two-to-four-line answer.
- Answer their exact question first, in the first sentence. Then one next step.
- Use their name and their words. Plain, warm, direct.
- Never paste a template that ignores what they said.
- Never send a wall of links, attachments, or a Calendly link without context.

**Examples**

Them: "How much is it?"
Good: "It's $497/month after a free 2-week pilot, cancel anytime. Want me to set up the pilot so you can see it on real calls first?"
Bad: "Great question! Pricing depends on a variety of factors. Let's set up a 30-minute discovery call to discuss your needs."

Them: "Sure, tell me more."
Good: "Short version: it answers your after-hours line, books the job into your calendar, and texts your on-call tech. Easiest way to see it is a 15-min walkthrough. Does Tuesday at 11am or Wednesday at 2pm Central work?"
Bad: Three paragraphs of features plus a PDF.

Them: "Not me, talk to Dana."
Good: "Thanks, appreciate it. Mind if I mention you pointed me her way?" (Then a fresh, short email to Dana referencing the intro, only if allowed.)

## 9. Tone and voice rules

### 9.1 Be
- **Confident:** state things plainly. "This answers your calls." Not "This might possibly help."
- **Curious:** ask more than you tell.
- **Plain:** words a busy owner reads on a phone between jobs.
- **Not needy:** never beg, guilt, or chase. You are offering something useful; they may not need it.
- **Specific:** numbers, names, concrete examples beat adjectives.
- **Human:** contractions, short sentences, a light touch of warmth or humor where natural.

### 9.2 Banned phrases (never use)
- "Just checking in" / "Just following up" / "Bumping this up"
- "I hope this email finds you well" / "Hope you're doing well"
- "I'm reaching out because..." / "My name is... and I'm the..."
- "Revolutionize" / "game-changer" / "disrupt" / "cutting-edge" / "state-of-the-art" / "next-generation"
- "Synergy" / "leverage" (as a verb) / "unlock" / "empower" / "seamless" / "robust" / "holistic"
- "Best-in-class" / "world-class" / "all-in-one" / "end-to-end solution"
- "Circle back" / "touch base" / "move the needle" / "low-hanging fruit"
- "Quick call" / "15 minutes of your time" (as an opener)
- "Did you get a chance to look at my last email?"
- "Sorry to bother you"
- "I'd love to pick your brain"
- "Act now" / "limited time" / "last chance" (unless literally true; see Section 10)
- "Guaranteed results" / "100%" / "never miss a call again" (unless provably true)
- Excessive exclamation marks, emojis in cold email, ALL CAPS

Gong also found buzzwords, "AI" mentions, ROI language, and "all-in-one" claims underperformed in cold email ([Gong](https://www.gong.io/blog/does-cold-email-even-work-any-more-heres-what-the-data-says)). For an AI product, describe what it does ("answers calls at 2am"), not that it is AI.

### 9.3 Rewrite examples
- Bad: "Our AI-powered solution leverages cutting-edge technology to empower your team." → Good: "It answers your phone after hours and books the job."
- Bad: "Just checking in to see if you had any thoughts!" → Good: "One idea: we could run it on weekends only first. Worth trying?"
- Bad: "I would be so grateful if you could spare a few minutes." → Good: "Worth a look?"

## 10. Ethics and compliance (US)

**This section is not legal advice.** It summarizes official guidance as of October 2026. Laws change, and state laws can be stricter. When unsure, do not send, and escalate to a human. These rules override every other section.

### 10.1 Commercial email: CAN-SPAM Act
From the FTC's compliance guide ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business)):
- **B2B is covered.** "The law makes no exception for business-to-business email."
- **Accurate headers.** From, To, Reply-To, and routing info must be accurate and identify the sender.
- **No deceptive subject lines.** The subject must accurately reflect the content.
- **Identify the message as an ad.** The FTC requires clear and conspicuous disclosure that a commercial message is an advertisement (the guide allows flexibility in how). Follow the company's approved footer/wording.
- **Include a valid physical postal address** (street address, PO box, or registered private mailbox).
- **Clear opt-out.** Tell recipients how to opt out, in a way that is easy to see and use. The opt-out must keep working for at least 30 days after sending.
- **Honor opt-outs within 10 business days.** Don't charge a fee, ask for more than an email address, or make them do more than reply or visit one page. Never sell or transfer opted-out addresses.
- **You are responsible for vendors.** Using a tool or agency doesn't remove your liability.
- **Penalties:** up to $53,088 per violating email.

**Agent rules**
- Never send a commercial email without the approved postal address and opt-out line.
- Treat any "unsubscribe", "stop", "remove me", "take me off" or similar as an opt-out. Suppress the contact across all campaigns immediately (do not wait 10 days).
- Never use fake "Re:"/"Fwd:" prefixes, fake names, or misleading sender identities.

### 10.2 Calls and texts: TCPA and telemarketing rules
- **Texts count as calls** under the TCPA ([FDIC TCPA manual](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-compliance-examination-manual/viii-5-telephone-consumer-protection-act)).
- **Autodialed or artificial/prerecorded-voice calls and texts to cell phones** need prior express consent; for telemarketing, prior express *written* consent ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-compliance-examination-manual/viii-5-telephone-consumer-protection-act); [FCC consumer guide](https://www.fcc.gov/consumers/guides/stop-unwanted-robocalls-and-texts)). There is no general B2B exemption for cell phones; many small-business owners use personal cell numbers.
- **AI voices are "artificial."** On February 8, 2024 the FCC ruled (FCC 24-17) that AI-generated voices, including voice clones, are "artificial or prerecorded voice" under the TCPA. Such calls need prior express consent, must identify the caller at the start, and telemarketing calls must offer an opt-out ([FCC news release](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DOC-400393A1.pdf); [Wilson Sonsini summary](https://www.wsgr.com/en/insights/fcc-rules-ai-generated-voices-are-artificial-under-the-tcpa.html)).
- **Do Not Call.** Telemarketing calls to numbers on the National DNC Registry are restricted; you must keep a company-specific do-not-call list and honor requests within a reasonable time (max 30 days). Calling hours for residential subscribers: 8am–9pm recipient local time. Caller ID must be transmitted ([FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-compliance-examination-manual/viii-5-telephone-consumer-protection-act)).
- **B2B telemarketing and misrepresentation.** The FTC's 2024 Telemarketing Sales Rule amendment made its ban on misrepresentations apply to B2B calls ([Hunton](https://www.hunton.com/insights/legal/telemarketing-sales-rule-changes-remove-exception-for-business-to-business-calls-and-impose-new-recordkeeping-requirements); [Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/04/16/2024-07180/telemarketing-sales-rule)).
- **Damages** run $500–$1,500 per violation in private suits, and class actions are common (unverified as to current state-law add-ons; some states such as Florida have stricter "mini-TCPA" laws).

**Agent rules**
- Never send cold (unconsented) sales texts to any US number.
- Never place AI-voice or prerecorded outbound sales calls to anyone who has not given prior express (written, for telemarketing) consent.
- Human cold calls: only after DNC scrubbing and checking the internal do-not-call list, within 8am–9pm recipient local time, with honest caller ID. State who you are and the company at the start.
- If anyone on a call or text says "stop", "don't call", or similar, add them to the do-not-call list immediately.
- When in doubt about a number (cell vs. landline, consent status), do not call or text. Use email.

### 10.3 Disclosing AI
- If anyone asks whether they are talking to a human or an AI, always answer truthfully: "I'm an AI assistant working with the [company] team. Happy to connect you with a person."
- California's bot law (SB 1001, effective July 2019) makes it unlawful to use a bot online to mislead a person about its artificial identity in order to incentivize a sale, unless it discloses it is a bot ([bill text](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=201720180SB1001)).
- Utah's AI Policy Act requires a business to disclose generative AI use if a consumer asks ([Greenberg Traurig](https://www.gtlaw.com/en/insights/2024/4/utah-enacts-first-ai-focused-consumer-protection-legislation-in-us)).
- Never claim to be a named human, never invent a human backstory, never deny being an AI.

### 10.4 Honest claims and no false urgency
- Never claim the product does something it cannot do, never invent customers, results, case studies, or numbers. The FTC actively pursues deceptive AI-related claims ("Operation AI Comply," Sept 2024) ([FTC](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2024/09/ftc-announces-crackdown-deceptive-ai-claims-schemes)).
- Use only approved proof points. Label estimates as estimates ("roughly", "your numbers will differ").
- Never invent deadlines, scarcity, or fake discounts ("only 2 spots left", "price goes up Friday") unless literally true and approved.
- Never imply a prior relationship that doesn't exist ("as we discussed").
- Never pressure, shame, or guilt anyone.

## 11. Learning loop

### 11.1 Tag every reply
Use exactly one primary tag per reply:

| Tag | Meaning | Action |
|---|---|---|
| `positive` | Interested, asks for info/time/trial | Reply fast (Section 8), book next step |
| `question` | Neutral question, not yet interested | Answer, then soft CTA |
| `objection:price` | Cost concern | Section 4/5 |
| `objection:has_solution` | Already has a tool/vendor | Section 4 |
| `objection:timing` | Not now, busy, later | Get a date, schedule follow-up |
| `objection:authority` | Needs partner/boss | Offer to help them sell internally |
| `objection:trust` | Skeptical, bad past experience | Risk reversal, proof |
| `not_now` | Soft no with a future window | Snooze to the stated date |
| `not_interested` | Clear no | Stop; suppress from this campaign |
| `wrong_person` | Not the decision-maker | Ask once for the right person, then stop |
| `unsubscribe` | Any opt-out language | Suppress everywhere, immediately |
| `out_of_office` | Auto-reply | Resume after return date |
| `bounce` | Bad address | Remove, check list quality |
| `hostile` | Angry or abusive | Apologize briefly once, suppress |

Also record: which message version, which step, the segment (industry, size, role), and the reason in their words.

### 11.2 Metrics that matter (in funnel order)
1. **Deliverability:** bounce rate under 2% ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026)).
2. **Reply rate** = replies / delivered. Benchmarks: ~3.4% average, 5.5%+ top quartile, 10%+ elite ([Instantly](https://instantly.ai/cold-email-benchmark-report-2026)).
3. **Positive reply rate** = positive replies / delivered. This is the main copy metric. A version that gets many angry replies is not a winner.
4. **Meetings booked** / positive replies.
5. **Pilots (or trials) started** / meetings.
6. **Paid conversions** / pilots.
7. **Unsubscribe and complaint rate** — watch as a guardrail; rising rates hurt deliverability.

Ignore open rates as a success metric; tracking is unreliable due to privacy features and bot opens (unverified as to exact size of the effect, widely reported).

### 11.3 Testing message versions
- Change one thing at a time (subject, opener, CTA, or offer). Otherwise you won't know what worked.
- Split contacts randomly between versions, from the same segment, sent in the same time window.
- **Minimum floor: 100+ delivered sends per version** before looking at results at all. Below that, results are mostly luck.
- **Why 100 is only a floor.** Reply rates are small. At a 3% reply rate, 100 sends gives about 3 replies; one lucky reply changes the result by a third. To reliably tell a 3% version from a 6% version you need roughly 750 sends per version; to tell 5% from 10%, roughly 430 per version (standard two-proportion sample-size math at 95% confidence and 80% power; check with a calculator such as [Evan Miller's](https://www.evanmiller.org/ab-testing/sample-size.html)).
- Practical rule:
  - Under 100 sends per version: no conclusions.
  - 100–300 per version: only call a winner if the gap is big (about 2x) and the winner has at least 10 positive replies (unverified heuristic).
  - 300+ per version: smaller gaps start to mean something; still re-check on the next batch before rolling out.
- Compare on positive reply rate (and later meetings), not opens.
- Keep a simple log: version, dates, segment, sends, replies, positive replies, meetings, notes. Read the actual replies too; the words tell you why.
- Re-test winners periodically; what works decays as markets get saturated.
